Ratchet · a data essay on weight, willpower, and biology

The body
fights back.

Most popular diets perform about the same. Calories in, calories out is real but incomplete. And the cruelest finding is this: weight behaves like a ratchet, easy to click upward and stubbornly hard to let back down, because once a person loses weight the body fights for years to put it back. Here is what the evidence actually shows, and why "just eat less" misses how steep the climb really is.

The premise · Do the diets work

No diet has a secret engine.

My skepticism here was well placed. When researchers put the famous diets head to head and control them properly, the branded approach almost never matters. What matters is whether a person can stick to it.

The cleanest test is Stanford's DIETFITS trial: 609 adults randomized to a healthy low-fat or a healthy low-carb diet for a year. Both groups lost roughly the same amount, around five to six kilograms, and matching people to a diet by their genes or insulin response gave no edge. The earlier POUNDS LOST trial varied fat, protein, and carbohydrate ratios and found weight loss did not track the macros. A large review of the named commercial diets reached the same verdict: similar modest losses across all of them, with adherence as the real predictor.

So why does keto feel like magic in week one?

Because most of that first whoosh is water, not fat. The body stores carbohydrate as glycogen, and every gram of glycogen holds roughly three to four grams of water with it. Cut carbs and the body drains that reservoir fast, dropping several pounds that have nothing to do with body fat. When metabolism is measured in tightly controlled feeding studies, low-carb diets show no meaningful "fat-burning advantage." Any difference is on the order of a few dozen calories a day, small enough to vanish into measurement noise. Keto can absolutely help a given person, but through appetite and structure, not a metabolic loophole.

The machinery · Calories in, calories out

True. And not the whole story.

Energy balance governs weight. No one loses fat without a net deficit; that part is essentially physics. The trap is treating it like a simple thermostat, where both numbers sit still and only deliberate choices move them. They do not sit still.

"Calories out" is not one number. It is resting metabolism (the largest piece), the energy spent digesting food, deliberate exercise, and all the fidgeting and movement no one thinks about. Every one of those can shrink when a person eats less. "Calories in" is not fully under conscious control either. How full a food leaves someone, how fast it gets eaten, and how much the body absorbs all shift with what is on the plate.

A tightly controlled feeding study showed how much the food itself matters. With calories, sugar, fat, and fiber matched, people allowed to eat freely consumed about 500 more calories a day on an ultra-processed menu than on an unprocessed one, and gained weight, simply because processed food is easy to eat quickly and slow to satisfy. Same calories on paper, very different outcomes in a body.

The one lever with real leverage is protein. It costs more energy to digest than carbohydrate or fat, and it is the most filling of the three, which quietly nudges both sides of the equation in a favorable direction. That is most of the practical truth behind every diet that works: it is a delivery system for eating somewhat less, somewhat more comfortably, for long enough to matter.

The trap · Why it is so hard to keep off

Lose the weight, and the body treats it as an emergency.

This is the part that deserves empathy, because it is not a story about weak character. It is a story about a survival system doing exactly what it evolved to do, and doing it to people who are trying as hard as they can.

Follow the contestants from "The Biggest Loser". The sample is small, just fourteen, and extreme, rapid loss under a television regimen, so take it as a vivid illustration rather than proof on its own. They lost an average of 58 kilograms. Their resting metabolism fell with it, which is what anyone would expect for a smaller body. But six years later, after most had regained much of the weight, their metabolism had not recovered. It was still running about 700 calories a day below where they started, and roughly 500 of those calories were unexplained by their body size alone. Their bodies were burning far less fuel than a person of the same weight who had never dieted. They were, in effect, being penalized for having lost weight.

The people who kept the most weight off were the ones whose metabolism slowed the most. The harder the success, the harder the body pushed back.

And the hunger does not switch off either.

Metabolism is only half the defense. In a study tracking appetite hormones through weight loss, the changes were still there a full year later. Ghrelin, the hormone that drives hunger, stayed elevated. Leptin and the gut hormones that signal fullness stayed suppressed. And the people reported being measurably hungrier than before they ever lost the weight. The body was not just burning less. It was actively turning up the pressure to eat, and keeping it turned up.

Put the two together and the regain statistics stop looking like failures of discipline. Across long-term studies, people regain on the order of 80 percent of their lost weight within five years. Only about one in five keeps off a meaningful amount for the long haul. That is not a population of people who stopped caring. It is a population fighting a body that lowered its fuel use and raised its hunger and then waited, patiently, for willpower to run out.

This is the nuance I was after, the ground between two bad slogans. "People can't lose weight" is false; people lose weight all the time. "Just eat less and move more" is technically true and almost useless, because it describes the task while ignoring that the body rewrites the rules the moment a person starts. Sustained weight loss is not easy work that some people are too lazy to do. It is genuinely hard work against an opponent that gets stronger the more it loses. Treating it as a character flaw is both wrong on the science and cruel in the doing.

The asymmetry · why prevention beats reversal

Going up is a slope. Coming back is a wall.

The defense I just walked through is lopsided. The body fights weight loss far harder than it fights weight gain. That single fact is why the cheapest pound to lose is the one I never put on.

Researchers call it asymmetric regulation. Lose weight and the body mounts a full counterattack of hunger and slowed metabolism. Gain weight and the pushback is weak and easily overridden. One obesity researcher summed it up bluntly: "obesity protects obesity." The lower edge of my weight is guarded like a border. The upper edge is a screen door.

And it gets more durable than appetite and metabolism alone. Fat cells are the storage tanks, and their number is mostly set by early adulthood and barely changes after that. When I gain, those cells swell, and a long enough surplus can even add new ones. Here is the catch the research is clear on: when I lose the weight, the cells shrink, they do not disappear. I am left with the same set of tanks, or more, now sitting empty and biologically primed to refill. A body that has been heavier keeps the hardware to get there again.

Lose the fat, keep the tanks

Fat-cell number holds steady; only the size changes

Source: Spalding et al., Nature 2008. Fat-cell number is set largely in childhood and adolescence and stays roughly constant in adulthood (about 10% renewed per year), with the count holding even after major weight loss. Cells shrink during weight loss rather than vanishing.

Now my own numbers.

I spent my life at six foot two and 165. When I boxed and lifted, I climbed to about 175, then drifted right back to 165 once I stopped. That is the asymmetry working in my favor: 165 was the weight my body defended, so a short trip above it simply sagged back down on its own. I never had to fight for it.

This past year I have added around 20 pounds, some of it muscle, and I am sitting near 185. At six foot two that is a healthy, filled-out place to be, and I think the added muscle suits me. The thing worth understanding is what happens next if my habits slip and the number keeps climbing.

How to read it: The dashed line is the weight my body actively defends. For years it sat at 165, which is why the climb to 175 slid back on its own. If a higher weight is held long enough, that defended line ratchets up to meet it, and it does not come back down easily. The bracket on the right is the uphill climb I would be signing up for if I ever decided I wanted to be leaner again.

If 185 drifts to 200 and 200 sits there for a year or two, my body starts treating 200 as the number to protect. And if I then decided I wanted to get back to 165, those 35 pounds would stop behaving like my easy 175-to-165 slide and start behaving like the Biggest Loser climb from the last section: hunger turned up, metabolism turned down, the full defense, all of it aimed at keeping me exactly where I had decided I no longer wanted to be.

The drift from 185 to 200 would be gravity. The climb back down would be rock climbing. They are not the same trip in reverse.

None of this is destiny, and it is not a reason for alarm at 185. Set point is a model, not a law. Environment and habits move it, muscle is not fat, and a lifelong-lean, athletic body that only recently filled out is about the best possible starting position I could ask for. That is exactly the point. The window where a higher weight has not yet become my defended weight is open right now, and it is the cheapest it will ever be to stay near a weight my body still recognizes as home. The takeaway is not panic. It is that prevention is not merely easier than reversal, it is a different and gentler kind of effort entirely. A little vigilance now spares me the enormous, biology-defying version later.

The lever · What GLP-1s change

The first tool that fights back against the body.

If the core problem is a biology that defends fat with hunger and a slowed metabolism, then the shift with GLP-1 medications is that they reach into that biology directly, instead of asking people to out-discipline it.

Drugs like semaglutide (Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Zepbound) mimic and amplify the gut hormones that tell the brain it is full. They blunt appetite, quiet the constant food noise, and slow how fast the stomach empties. In effect they push back on the exact hunger signal that defeats dieters in the Sumithran study. That is why the results are on a different scale from anything diet alone has produced.

A different order of magnitude

Average weight loss in trials · percent of body weight

Source: STEP-1 (semaglutide, 14.9%, NEJM 2021); SURMOUNT-1 (tirzepatide, 22.5%); SURMOUNT-5 head-to-head (tirzepatide 20.2% vs semaglutide 13.7%, 2025); retatrutide phase-2 (~24%, investigational). Lifestyle figure is typical maintained loss from behavioral programs.

But the same studies carry the lesson of this whole essay. When people stop the medication, the defended biology comes right back. In the semaglutide extension, patients regained about two thirds of their lost weight within a year of stopping. The drug was not a cure that resets the body; it was a counterweight that only works while it is held. That is why clinicians increasingly describe obesity the way they describe high blood pressure: a chronic condition that the treatment manages rather than ends.

What happens when the counterweight is removed

Body weight change · on treatment, then after stopping

Source: Wilding et al., STEP-1 extension, Diabetes Obesity & Metabolism 2022. After withdrawal, participants regained roughly two thirds of the lost weight within a year, settling about 5.6% below their starting point.

There are real caveats worth holding alongside the optimism. A meaningful share of the weight lost on these drugs is lean tissue, not just fat, which is why protein and strength training matter even more on them. Cost and access are uneven, side effects are common during dose escalation, and the long-run picture is still being written. None of that erases the central point: for the first time, the thing being treated is the biology itself, not the patient's resolve.

The honest middle

Hard is not the same as impossible.

Hold all of it at once. The fancy diets are mostly interchangeable, and the one that works is the one a person can actually live with. Calories in and calories out is true at the foundation, but both sides move the moment someone starts, and the food on the plate changes how much gets eaten without any decision to eat more. Losing weight is achievable. Keeping it off means living, often permanently, against a body that has quietly turned down its furnace and turned up its hunger, and is in no rush to stop.

That is the empathy the data earns. Not the despair of "nothing works," and not the contempt of "just try harder," but the respect owed to anyone doing one of the genuinely hard things a human body can be asked to do, against an opponent that does not get tired. The people who manage it are not displaying ordinary discipline. They are overriding a survival system, day after day. And the people who do not manage it are not failing a test of worth. They are losing a fight that was rigged at the level of hormones and heat.

Worthiness was never the variable. Biology was.
